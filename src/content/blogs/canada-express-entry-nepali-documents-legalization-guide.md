Introduction

Canada's Express Entry system runs on a simple idea: the government ranks candidates by a Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) score and invites the highest scorers to apply for permanent residence. What trips up most Nepali applicants isn't the scoring — it's what happens after you get an Invitation to Apply (ITA). Suddenly you have a strict deadline to produce a stack of documents that prove your education, your background, and your identity in a format Canadian immigration officers can actually verify.

This is where a huge number of applications stall. A transcript request that takes six weeks in Nepal. A police certificate that has to be requested through the right office. A translation that gets rejected because it wasn't done the way IRCC expects. None of this is complicated once you know the sequence — but it does need to start well before you're sitting on a 60-day ITA clock.

Key Requirements: What Actually Needs to Be Prepared

1. Educational Credential Assessment (ECA) — usually through WES If any part of your education was completed outside Canada, your degree doesn't automatically count toward your CRS score. You need an Educational Credential Assessment from an organization IRCC designates — most commonly World Education Services (WES), though CES, ICAS, IQAS, and ICES are also recognized. The ECA converts your Nepali degree into its Canadian equivalent and directly affects your education points: a three-year bachelor's can be worth up to 120 CRS points, a master's up to 135. Skip this step, or get it wrong, and you could be leaving significant points on the table — or be ineligible for the Federal Skilled Worker Program outright, since an ECA is mandatory for FSW principal applicants.

2. Police Clearance Certificate (PCC) from Nepal IRCC requires a police certificate from every country where you've lived six consecutive months or more, since the age of 18, within the last 10 years. For most Nepali applicants, that means a PCC from Nepal is required regardless of where you currently live.

3. Certified translations for any document not in English or French Birth certificates, marriage certificates, academic transcripts, and police certificates issued in Nepali must be translated. IRCC is specific about how this has to be done — a translation from a friend or family member, however accurate, will be rejected.

4. Civil documents — birth certificate, marriage certificate (if applicable) These support identity and family composition in your application and, again, need translation if not already in English.

Step-by-Step Process

Step 1: Start your WES ECA application early — ideally before you even enter the Express Entry pool Create a WES account, select "Immigration (IRCC)" as your purpose, and choose Course-by-Course evaluation (the type IRCC generally expects). WES will generate a reference number you'll need for the next step.

Step 2: Request your transcript from your university with your WES reference number attached For Tribhuvan University graduates, this means visiting the Office of the Controller of Examinations to request your transcript be prepared and sent as an official, sealed document. Nepali universities generally require the transcript to be sent directly by the institution — not carried by you — for WES to accept it as official. Processing at TU and similar institutions can take several weeks, which is exactly why this step should start as early as possible, not after your ITA arrives.

Step 3: Upload your degree certificate to your WES profile Unlike transcripts, your final degree certificate is typically uploaded by you as a scanned copy through your WES account.

Step 4: Wait for your WES report — then link it to your Express Entry profile Once WES receives your transcripts and completes its review, it issues an evaluation report. This typically takes a few weeks after all documents arrive, so build that into your timeline before you expect an ITA.

Step 5: Apply for your Nepal Police Clearance Certificate This is handled through Nepal Police's Crime Investigation Department. If you're currently in Nepal, you (or an authorized person on your behalf) can apply in person or through the department's verification process. If you're already abroad, the request is generally made through the Nepali embassy or consulate, or directly in writing to Police Headquarters in Kathmandu. Because processing and courier time can add up, this should be requested as soon as you're actively building your Express Entry profile — not after your ITA deadline starts the clock.

Step 6: Get certified translations of every Nepali-language document For each document not already in English, IRCC requires either:

A translation from a certified translator (a member in good standing of a recognized translators' association), which is generally accepted without further notarization, or
A translation from an independent translator accompanied by a sworn affidavit confirming accuracy, if a certified translator isn't available for Nepali.

Translations must be complete and word-for-word — including seals, stamps, and handwritten notes — and must be submitted alongside a copy of the original document. IRCC explicitly does not accept translations done by the applicant or a family member, regardless of their language skill.

Step 7: Submit your Express Entry profile and wait for your ITA With your ECA and language test scores in hand, you can enter the pool. Once you receive an Invitation to Apply, you typically have 60 days to submit your full application — which is why every document above should already be in hand or in process well before this point.

Step 8: Compile and submit your application package This includes your ECA report, language test results, police certificate, translated civil documents, proof of funds, and any other program-specific documents. Double-check names, dates, and document numbers match exactly across every file — mismatches are one of the most common reasons applications face delays.

Common Mistakes That Delay Nepali Applications
Requesting the PCC or transcript only after receiving the ITA — both can take weeks, and the 60-day window doesn't leave much room for error
Submitting a translation without the required affidavit, when the translator isn't a certified member of a recognized association
Sending a personal copy of a transcript instead of one sent directly and sealed by the university
Assuming a PCC with no expiry date is automatically valid — IRCC has its own rules about how recent a certificate must be, separate from what's printed on the document
Mismatched names across passport, degree certificate, and marriage or birth certificates (common when Nepali documents use slightly different name spellings or formats)
