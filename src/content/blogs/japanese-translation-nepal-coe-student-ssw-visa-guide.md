## Introduction

If you are applying to a Japanese language school, a university or a Specified Skilled Worker (SSW) job, someone will eventually ask you for "translated documents." The advice online is confusing. Some pages say every page must be notarized and stamped by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MoFA). Others say anything goes.

The truth sits in between, and it depends on **who is reading your file**. This guide explains how the Japanese system works, which documents usually need translation, and how to avoid paying for steps nobody asked for.

## The Two Stages Most Applicants Miss

**Stage 1: The Certificate of Eligibility (COE).** Your sponsor in Japan, such as your school or employer, applies for the COE with Japanese immigration. You send them documents from Nepal. This is where most translation is needed.

**Stage 2: The visa.** Once you hold the COE, you apply for the visa. In Nepal, Japan visa applications go through the Japan Visa Application Centre in Kathmandu (VFS Global). That stage has a much shorter checklist, usually the application form, passport, photo, the COE and a few school documents. Extra translations are asked only for specific categories, such as company registration documents for certain skilled-labour cases.

## What Japan's Side Actually Asks For

University and school COE instructions, which follow immigration rules, repeat the same points:

* A document that is **not in Japanese or English** needs a translation attached.
* The translation must show the **translator's name and the date**. Some schools also ask for the translator's relationship to the applicant.
* The translation should be **typed, not handwritten**.
* **Notarization is not required** by several universities that state this directly.
* Financial documents are usually accepted only if issued **within three months** of submission.

Schools differ. Some accept Japanese or English translations, while some programs ask for Japanese translations even for documents written in English. **Your school's checklist always wins.**

## Myth vs. Fact

| Common claim | What the sources say |
|---|---|
| "Every document must be in Japanese." | Many schools accept Japanese or English, but a few require Japanese. Ask your school. |
| "You need a Nepal notary and MoFA stamp." | Several Japanese universities say notarization is not required. Do these steps only if your school, employer or an authority asks. |
| "The embassy reviews your sponsor documents." | Sponsor documents go to the school or employer for the COE. The visa stage uses a short checklist. |
| "Any translation is fine." | It must be complete, typed, and show the translator's name and date. |

## Documents Commonly Translated

Schools and employers commonly ask for the following. Confirm the exact list with your sponsor.

* **Financial sponsor documents:** bank balance certificate, income certificate, tax clearance certificate
* **Relationship documents:** relationship certificate and birth certificate
* **Applicant documents:** citizenship certificate, academic certificates and transcripts
* **Experience letters and skill certificates** for working-visa routes
* **SSW applicants:** Japanese-language and skills-test results, as requested by the employer

For SSW, the applicant must pass a skills test and show basic Japanese, through the JLPT N4 or JFT-Basic. The employer files the COE, so ask them which translations they need.

## Step-by-Step Process

1. **Get the sponsor's checklist** from your school or employer.
2. **Note the language** they accept: Japanese, English, or Japanese only.
3. **Check document dates.** Order fresh financial documents so they fall within the accepted period.
4. **Translate everything on the page.** Include seals, stamps, serial numbers and handwritten notes.
5. **Convert dates carefully.** Show the original Bikram Sambat (BS) date and its AD equivalent. Japan works in the Gregorian calendar.
6. **Add the translator's name and date** to each translation.
7. **Do a consistency check.** Names, dates and amounts must match across the sponsor's income, tax and relationship documents and your passport.

## Common Mistakes

* BS to AD errors that create mismatches with the passport
* Different spellings of the same name across documents
* Skipping seals, stamps and notes
* Using expired financial documents
* Handwritten translations or translations without the translator's name and date
* Paying for notary or MoFA stamps your school never asked for
